Learn / DNA tests

Your DNA test says “Jewish.” What now?

Maybe it was a percentage you didn’t expect, a cousin match, or a Sephardic label that finally explained an old family custom. Here is what a result does and doesn’t mean, and the calm next steps.

General information only. Whether a person is Jewish is decided by a rabbi, never by a test or by us.

First: you don’t have to do anything today

A surprise like this can stir up a lot. There is no deadline and no step you are expected to take right away. Take time to take it in, talk with your family, and ask questions when you are ready.

What a test can tell you

Consumer DNA tests estimate where your ancestors came from, sometimes with a label such as “Ashkenazi Jewish” or “Sephardic Jewish,” and they connect you with relatives who share your DNA. For many people that’s the first sign of a family story nobody told them.

What it can’t

  • A percentage measures ancestry, not status. It doesn’t say which side of your family it came from.
  • It can’t show an unbroken line through your mother, her mother and so on, which is what Jewish law (halacha) looks at.
  • A small percentage doesn’t rule anything out. It can come from one grandparent or great-grandparent.

So a result, on its own, isn’t accepted as proof of Jewish status, and it doesn’t disprove it either. Rabbis rely on family records and testimony, and a test is often what leads you to them.

Only your father’s side? Ask anyway.

Jewish law follows the mother’s line. But many people who think their Jewish roots are only on their father’s side, or long ago, turn out to have a Jewish mother’s line too. Often it is just two or three generations back: a grandmother or great-grandmother whose family nobody kept track of. You can’t know until you look, so it is always worth asking.

If your roots are Sephardic

Many people in Latin America, Spain, Portugal and the Caribbean find Sephardic ancestry. Some of it goes back to the Jews of Spain and Portugal who were forced to give up their faith more than 500 years ago, known as anusim. Some families kept quiet customs for generations: lighting candles on Friday night, covering mirrors after a death, not mixing certain foods. Other families came later and simply stopped talking about being Jewish.

Either way, your line may be closer and clearer than you think. A rabbi can help you follow it and tell you where you stand.

If it may be your mother’s line

If your mother’s mother was Jewish, and her mother before her, then by Jewish law you are Jewish, whatever you were raised as and whether or not anyone told you. See a Jewish mother for the kinds of records that help show it.

Calm next steps

  1. Talk to family. Ask older relatives about names, places, customs and anything that was kept quiet. Write down what they say.
  2. Gather documents. Birth, marriage and death records, religious certificates, old letters, gravestones, immigration papers. Use your DNA matches to find relatives who may have them.
  3. Talk to a rabbi. An Orthodox rabbi near you can look at what you found and tell you what it means. If you don’t know one, ask us and we will try to help you find someone.
  4. Take it one step at a time. Whatever the rabbi finds, there is no pressure, and you are welcome to keep asking questions.

Where brit milah comes in

If you are Jewish and never had a brit milah, it is a mitzvah you can still do at any age. See is it too late? and brit milah for adults. You are welcome to ask us about it at any stage, even while you are still finding out.
